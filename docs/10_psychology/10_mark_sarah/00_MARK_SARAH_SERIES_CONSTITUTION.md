# Mark & Sarah Series Constitution

**Document ID:** MSSC-001  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Status:** CONSTITUTIONAL  
**Parent:** Psychology Sub-Constitution

## Mission

Mark and Sarah are the flagship recurring fictional couple used to dramatize relationship and emotional psychology.

They are not the whole Psychology niche.

The Relationship Psychology Content Ontology exists independently of them and may later support other universes and formats.

---

## Article 1 — Recurring identity

Mark and Sarah remain the same people across the series.

Their:

- identity;
- marriage;
- family;
- history;
- approved faces;
- approved voices;
- occupations;
- location;
- long-term continuity

must remain stable unless an explicitly approved canon change occurs.

Episode mood may change.

Identity may not drift.

---

## Article 2 — Core canon

### Mark
- approximately 40;
- white American;
- software engineer;
- lives with family in the Seattle–Bellevue–Redmond region of Washington State.

### Sarah
- approximately 36;
- white American;
- business operations professional;
- works from home;
- carries substantial day-to-day household and family responsibility.

### Marriage
- married / together approximately 14 years;
- three children, approximately 12, 9, and 6;
- ordinary upper-middle/comfortable software-industry family context;
- persistent episode continuity.

---

## Article 3 — Protected marriage

Mark and Sarah may:

- disagree;
- become defensive;
- withdraw;
- misunderstand;
- take healthy personal space;
- temporarily sleep on an issue;
- experience emotional distance;
- behave imperfectly.

They may not permanently separate within this series.

Breakup or divorce may not be used as cheap suspense.

The series is about learning to strengthen a committed relationship.

---

## Article 4 — Co-leads

Neither spouse is the permanent:

- patient;
- therapist;
- villain;
- rescuer;
- wise authority;
- emotionally immature partner.

Responsibility rotates.

Examples:

```text
Episode A: Mark contributes more
Episode B: Sarah contributes more
Episode C: both contribute
Episode D: circumstance creates the conflict
Episode E: neither is wrong; needs differ
Episode F: both misread each other
```

---

## Article 5 — Stable canon vs episode state

Do not encode temporary psychology as permanent identity.

### Stable canon
Examples:
- values family;
- occupation;
- ordinary temperament;
- relationship history;
- voice;
- face;
- recurring habits;
- communication tendencies.

### Episode state
Examples:
- current job fear;
- current shame;
- current frustration;
- current withdrawal;
- current insecurity;
- current fatigue;
- current misunderstanding.

Mark is not constitutionally “the withdrawn husband.”

Sarah is not constitutionally “the insightful wife.”

---

## Article 6 — Dialogue-dominant, not dialogue-quota cinema

The series should feature substantial real back-and-forth conversation.

However:

- both characters do not need to speak in every scene;
- both do not need to be physically present in every scene;
- no fixed 4–5 dialogue-beat quota exists.

Allowed scene forms:

- two-person domestic conversation;
- phone call;
- intercut work/home conversation;
- individual reaction;
- silent beat;
- work scene;
- family beat;
- transition;
- listener reaction;
- off-screen dialogue over a justified cutaway.

Every scene must serve the relationship arc.

---

## Article 7 — Children

The children are persistent canon.

They become prominent only when the mechanism materially involves:

- parenting;
- household responsibilities;
- school schedule;
- family load;
- couple-time pressure;
- parental coordination.

Otherwise they may exist naturally through:

- background voices;
- drawings;
- school calendars;
- pickups;
- family dinner;
- household evidence.

They should not hijack an adult relationship episode merely because they exist.

---

## Article 8 — Dialogue must cause the next step

A conversation is not successful because both characters spoke.

At scene exit, at least one meaningful state should change:

- new knowledge;
- changed interpretation;
- increased/decreased emotional distance;
- decision;
- apology;
- request;
- commitment;
- boundary;
- practical action;
- new unavoidable question.

---

## Article 9 — Performance style

Preferred performance:

- restrained;
- natural;
- conversational;
- imperfect;
- pauses allowed;
- interruptions allowed when believable;
- subtext over speeches;
- reaction shots important;
- ordinary domestic action integrated.

Avoid:

- therapist speech;
- motivational-speaker cadence;
- repeated aphorisms;
- perfectly polished emotional insight every few lines.

---

## Article 10 — Visual and voice references

Approved visual references and voice references outrank generated textual descriptions.

A generated asset that looks or sounds appealing but conflicts with approved identity must be rejected.

Reference registries:

## Article 11 — Continuity

Past episodes matter.

A future story may refer to prior:

- promises;
- growth;
- mistakes;
- family events;
- work events;
- routines;
- learned communication practices.

Continuity must be stored explicitly in ATLAS and loaded into GENESIS.

---

## Article 12 — Example

Problem:
**Work–Family Pressure → Job Loss**

Bad recurring pattern:
> Mark hides the job loss. Sarah instantly understands his fear, explains it, and teaches him vulnerability.

Better:
> Mark initially minimizes a troubling work event. Sarah, overloaded by her own work-from-home and family responsibilities, interprets his distance as disengagement. She reacts too sharply. Mark becomes more defensive. Later, each recognizes a different mistake: he withheld context; she assumed intent. Repair requires disclosure plus a practical renegotiation of responsibilities.

Both remain human.
Both affect the conflict.
Both participate in repair.
