# Richard Yu — 35 Digital Product Ideas (Structured List)

**Source:** https://youtu.be/YeHnafhoclE — "I have too many digital product ideas (35+ to steal)"
**Author:** Richard Yu
**Core rule:** *It's not the product that makes money. It's who you make it for and how you package it.*
**Six buckets:** Templates → Small Software → AI Capability → Teaching → Recurring/Community → The Big Two.

---

## Bucket 1 — Templates (Ideas 1–8)

> The money in templates was never the format. It's the specificity.

### 1. Calculator for a scary expensive decision
- **What:** A spreadsheet that helps someone make a decision they're terrified of getting wrong — rental property analyzer, house-flip profit calculator, pricing calculator for a small online store.
- **Why it works:** A budget tracker protects $40; a rental property calculator protects a $200,000 decision. Same effort to build, but you charge $10 vs $99. Specialized spreadsheets sell for $79–$200 while generic ones fight over $10.

### 2. Occupation-specific version of a generic template
- **What:** Build ONE expense tracker, then sell it as the Uber driver expense tracker, the Etsy seller expense tracker, the Airbnb host expense tracker. Same skeleton, change labels, add 3–4 job-specific fields.
- **Why it works:** When someone types their job into a search bar and your product says their exact job back to them, it feels made just for them. You're not competing with 5,000 generic trackers — you're the only one that named them. Less competition, higher price.

### 3. Swipe file that's a fill-in-the-blank, not a museum
- **What:** Take proven examples (e.g. 50 great emails) and bracket every variable — name, offer, deadline. Now it's a fill-in-the-blank the buyer finishes over a coffee.
- **Why it works:** A plain swipe file is inspiration that sits in a folder. You're not selling the words anymore — you're selling the removal of the blank page, which is what people actually get stuck on.

### 4. Brand kit in a box for one single industry
- **What:** Everything a brand-new candle company needs to not look like a beginner — logo, colors, fonts, first 10 Instagram posts. Pick one industry and go deep (bold/minimal for tech startups, warm/handmade for craft shops).
- **Why it works:** A logo template is a commodity. This is a shortcut to looking established on day one. The buyer sees themselves in it instantly; you charge for the transformation (looking legit), not a folder of files.

### 5. The bundle (a lever, not a product)
- **What:** Wrap existing products together — invoice template + expense tracker + P&L sheet = "Complete Small Business Finance Kit."
- **Why it works:** Sold separately they're $29 each and people buy one. Bundled it's $99 and feels like a steal — four things for $99 beats one thing for $29 in the buyer's mind. You didn't build anything new; you changed packaging and tripled the price.

### 6. Resume templates for one specific field
- **What:** Resumes built for a single field (nurse vs software engineer need totally different resumes).
- **Why it works:** Same specificity principle — the narrow one wins.

### 7. Landing page templates niched to one job
- **What:** A coaching site built to convert, not a blank canvas.
- **Why it works:** Niche-to-one-job beats generic.

### 8. Mock-up packs sold to other digital sellers
- **What:** Mock-up packs you sell to other digital sellers.
- **Why it works:** Selling shovels to the gold miners. Same principle — the narrow one wins.

## Bucket 2 — Small Software Tools (Ideas 9–15)

> The highest-margin product on the list — the wall that just came down (AI lets you build software in a weekend).

### 9. A one-button tool that kills one annoying task
- **What:** A tool that does exactly one thing — e.g. dumps tracking numbers from a spreadsheet straight into a store.
- **Why it works:** You're not selling software, you're selling the deletion of an annoying task. The narrower the task, the less competition — big companies think it's too small to bother with. That gap is yours.

### 10. A tool for a niche literally losing money on paper
- **What:** Tools for plumbers, dog groomers, cleaning companies — e.g. a plumber who lost $500 in a week from missing phone calls; a groomer who double-books; a cleaner assigning staff with sticky notes.
- **Why it works:** These people have money and a problem that costs them money, and almost nobody builds for them because it's not sexy. You stop the specific bleeding and price it like a business tool ($30–$80/mo), not a toy.

### 11. Vertical version of a tool that exists for everyone
- **What:** Take a generic tool (e.g. Linktree) and rebuild it only for musicians, real estate agents, or fitness coaches — every word, feature, and example speaks to that one person.
- **Why it works:** The most reliable pattern in software right now. You don't invent a new category — you take a tool that serves everybody and rebuild it to serve one type of person deeply. Software for everyone fails; software for fitness coaches wins. People pay more to be understood than served generically.

### 12. Collect the money first (before building)
- **What:** Make a one-page website describing the tool, put a Buy Now button on it, take it to 3 Facebook groups where the target hangs out, and watch. If people click to pay, you have a business — now build it. If nobody clicks, you saved 6 months.
- **Why it works:** One founder pre-sold 50 lifetime spots and collected $20,000 before writing a line of code. That's 50 people raising their hands saying "this should exist." Sell it first, build it second — never the other way around.

### 13. Launch hype waitlist for one kind of person
- **What:** A waitlist built for one specific type of person.
- **Why it works:** Quick-fire — same specificity principle.

### 14. Wall of love tool
- **What:** A tool that collects and displays testimonials in one place (people currently stitch it together with 3 different tools).
- **Why it works:** Quick-fire — removes a real annoyance.

### 15. Blog-post-to-social-week tool for a specific creator
- **What:** A tool that turns one blog post into a week of social posts for a specific type of creator who hates doing it manually.
- **Why it works:** Quick-fire — vertical + removes manual work.

## Bucket 3 — AI Capability (Ideas 16–21)

> The market moved from selling prompts ($5) to selling a capability ($200). Almost nobody's caught up.

### 16. Custom bot that does one expensive job
- **What:** A custom GPT loaded with knowledge, tuned and tested — e.g. pairing wine to a dinner menu, answering small business tax questions, explaining a confusing lease clause to a new landlord.
- **Why it works:** A prompt document sells for $19; a custom bot trained on a real body of knowledge sells for $200+. You're not selling instructions — you're selling a specialist that shows up already knowing the job.

### 17. The workflow, not the prompts
- **What:** Sell the whole assembly line, not one prompt. E.g. a podcaster pastes a transcript and gets a blog post + 5 social captions + an email — all four finished pieces.
- **Why it works:** Real work is a chain, not one step. Include example inputs and outputs so the buyer sees it working before they buy. People pay for certainty.

### 18. One-industry AI toolkit
- **What:** An AI toolkit for real estate agents — listing descriptions, buyer follow-up emails, negotiation scripts. The exact things that agent does every week.
- **Why it works:** Vertical beats general. The toolkit gets them in the door; a small subscription keeps workflows updated monthly; customization for their exact office is a service you charge real money for. One toolkit, three levels of client.

### 19. "Paste this in" bot setup
- **What:** A setup someone drops into their own accounts to instantly get a specialist.
- **Why it works:** Quick-fire — instant capability.

### 20. Updating prompt subscription for a niche
- **What:** A subscription for a niche that changes every month, with one free section as the hook.
- **Why it works:** Quick-fire — recurring + freshness.

### 21. The anti-mega pack
- **What:** A tight, tested pack that solves one narrow problem with sample outputs shown.
- **Why it works:** Focus always beats 1,000 prompts.

## Bucket 4 — Teaching (Ideas 22–27)

> What sells now isn't the big comprehensive course. It's the small thing that gets someone one specific result fast. Smaller is a strategy, not a compromise.

### 22. Unlock the cold start
- **What:** Find the first-step trap in a niche — the one wall that blocks everything else. E.g. a brand-new Airbnb host can't get their 6th review until they get their 1st. Build "the first five" — a 30-min product ($47) that gets a host from zero to five reviews (message an hour after check-in, a pre-arrival gesture, the one line that makes leaving a review effortless).
- **Why it works:** The new host is stuck against one wall; everything they want is on the other side. They'll happily pay $47 to get past the one thing blocking all of it. Same move works for a new freelancer with no clients, an author with no reviews.

### 23. The insider playbook from one narrow person
- **What:** Take a saturated niche and find the thing that actually keeps them up at night. E.g. wedding photographers — thousands of "how to take better photos" guides, but almost none on pricing. Build the Wedding Pricing Playbook: exact package tiers, the anchor number, the line to say when they push back.
- **Why it works:** Generic feels written for nobody; specific feels written for one person. The Pricing Playbook sells for 10x a generic ebook because the buyer reads the title and thinks "that is my exact problem." Same move: Pricing Playbook for freelance developers, First Hire Playbook for solo agency owners, What-to-Say-to-Insurance Playbook for therapists.

### 24. Sell the tool, not the lesson
- **What:** Ask: does this person need information or a tool? Information is a video on how to have a hard conversation; a tool is the word-for-word script they read off the screen on a Zoom call.
- **Why it works:** The buyer doesn't want to become an expert — they want the result without the homework. They'll pay more for the shortcut than the lesson every time because you're handing them their time back. Hand them the finished thing (script, plan, fill-in-the-blank) instead.

### 25. Free first-win mini lesson → paid one
- **What:** A free mini lesson that proves you can teach, leading into a paid one.
- **Why it works:** Quick-fire — prove you can teach before you ask for money.

### 26. AI for one specific job micro course
- **What:** "AI for real estate agents," not "Intro to AI."
- **Why it works:** Nobody wants AI in general; they want it for their job.

### 27. Swipe-and-deploy checklist for a one-time high-stakes moment
- **What:** Checklists for launching, hiring your first employee, filing taxes for the first time — moments people are terrified to get wrong.
- **Why it works:** Quick-fire — high-stakes, one-time, terrified-to-get-wrong moments.

## Bucket 5 — Recurring / Community (Ideas 28–33)

> The single biggest difference between people making a little money and people making real stable money: whether the money comes in once or every single month.

### 28. The support vacuum
- **What:** Build the community that lives in the gap where course buyers get stuck. E.g. people buy beginner woodworking courses then get stuck on their first project — start "The Workshop," $30/mo, promise: when you're stuck, post a photo and someone who's done it tells you what to do next.
- **Why it works:** People join on content but they can get any lesson free online. What they can't get is someone to answer their specific question at 11pm when they're stuck. They pay for the unstucking, not the information. You don't need to be the top expert — just run the room where people one step ahead help people one step behind.

### 29. The niche that sounds too small to be a business
- **What:** Calligraphy community — 1,200 members × $9/mo = $11,000/mo. Pickleball community — 1,400 members × $39/mo.
- **Why it works:** Passion does not care how small the niche is. A small obsessed audience beats a huge bored one every time, because obsession is what makes people stay — and staying is the entire game when money is recurring. The question isn't "is this niche big enough?" it's "is this niche obsessed enough?"

### 30. The desperate and underserved
- **What:** Find the group inside a giant market that everyone ignores. E.g. weight loss for women in menopause at $37/mo — not generic weight loss (hopeless to compete on), but one specific group at one specific life stage.
- **Why it works:** The generic version of a market can be the most crowded, miserable place to compete while the specific version of that exact same market is wide open. When an audience is specific and emotional, they're easier to reach and they stay longer.
- **Honest note:** A community is the opposite of hands-off — you have to show up. But that's exactly why it's hard to copy and why people who do it right build something that pays for years.

### 31. Gated network for one job title
- **What:** A gated network where the entry requirement itself is the value — "directors and above only."
- **Why it works:** Quick-fire — exclusivity is the product.

### 32. Free community → paid challenge
- **What:** A free community upfront that feeds a paid challenge every couple of months.
- **Why it works:** Quick-fire — free-to-paid funnel.

### 33. Accountability group around one measurable goal
- **What:** People show up because others are expecting them.
- **Why it works:** Quick-fire — social accountability drives retention.

## Bucket 6 — The Big Two (Ideas 34–35)

> The top of the mountain. Every small product you build is the on-ramp pointing toward these.

### 34. Sell the results, not a pile of stuff
- **What:** Once your small products have helped someone and they trust you, a small number have a much bigger problem. E.g. freelancers stuck at $50/hr who can't charge more → "The Rate Escape," a 90-day program moving them from hourly work to a productized service, via live calls + custom plan, priced at $5,000.
- **Why it works:** If it takes someone from $50/hr to $5,000/project, it pays for itself with the first clients. You're charging for a fraction of the results, not 90 days of your time. Nobody pays $5,000 for more stuff — they pay to make one painful, expensive problem go away. Price isn't about how much work you do; it's about how big the problem is.

### 35. Deliver it "done with you" so it doesn't eat your life
- **What:** Structure the high-ticket offer so the system does the teaching and your live access does the transformation. Backbone = the system you already built (templates, mini courses from earlier become the curriculum) + group calls, direct access, custom plans.
- **Why it works:** Two ways to get it wrong: (1) pure one-on-one → you burn out at a handful of clients; (2) fully hands-off pre-recorded videos → someone who pays $5k and gets a video library feels ripped off. The answer is the middle: your system teaches, your live access transforms. That's how you charge premium and keep your life. Every small product you built earlier isn't competing with this offer — it's the foundation underneath it. It was always one ladder.

## The One Piece of Advice That Matters Most

> Don't start with 35. Don't start with 10. **Pick one** — the single idea that made you go "wait, I could actually do that" — and build only that one. The people who win aren't the ones with the most ideas; they're the ones who actually finish the first one.
